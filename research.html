Breast imaging  |  Acad Radiol 2019 · Co-author; model developer

Breast cancer risk from the mammogram itself

Clinical problem
Breast density is a coarse risk marker. The question was whether a network reading the whole mammogram could stratify future risk better.
My contribution
I wrote the source code and programmed the CNN risk model, working in Columbia’s breast imaging AI group with Ha (first author) and Jambawalikar (senior author). This line of work led to my NIH StARR-funded CAPRI resident research year.
Approach
Retrospective case-control study: prior mammograms of 210 women who later developed cancer (420 images) vs. 527 controls (1,054 images). A fully convolutional network produced a pixel-wise risk map, with an 80/20 split and five-fold cross-validation.
Published finding
The network score and breast density were independent predictors. The network’s odds ratio was 4.42 (95% CI 3.4–5.7) vs. 1.67 (1.4–1.9) for density, with 72% accuracy in identifying the case group.
Limitation
Single institution, retrospective and relatively small. The authors expected more data to improve the model. A research model.

MSK · Pediatric  |  J Digit Imaging 2018 · First author

Machine-assisted bone age (MABAL)

Clinical problem
Bone-age reads against the Greulich–Pyle atlas are common, slow and observer-dependent. Earlier deep-learning attempts were limited by small datasets and generic architectures.
My contribution
First author, working with Columbia co-authors Chang, Ruzal-Shapiro and Ayyala.
Approach
10,289 hand radiographs from our PACS and the public Digital Hand Atlas, split into four sex-and-age cohorts. A custom 14-layer network with residual, inception and spatial-transformer layers was trained from scratch, with augmentation to limit overfitting.
Published finding
Aggregate mean absolute error of 0.637 years on validation and 0.536 years on the test set, where the reference standard averaged two pediatric radiologists. At publication, the best reported deep-learning result for bone age.
Limitation
One institution’s data plus a public atlas, and training labels came from single clinical reads. A research model, not a clinical product.

MSK · Emergency  |  J Digit Imaging 2020 · First author

Femoral neck fracture detection and Garden classification

Clinical problem
Hip fractures need to be found and classified quickly because displacement drives the surgical choice, but labelled examples, especially nondisplaced Garden I/II fractures, are scarce.
My contribution
First author, working with Columbia MSK co-authors Varada, Goel, Wong and Rasiej.
Approach
1,063 AP hip radiographs from 550 patients. One network localized the femoral neck and a second classified Garden I/II, Garden III/IV or no fracture. Training data were expanded to 9,063 images with GAN-generated radiographs and digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRRs) from preoperative hip CT.
Published finding
On 105 held-out real radiographs, fracture vs. no fracture reached AUC 0.92, accuracy 92.3%, sensitivity 0.91 and specificity 0.93. The three-class task reached AUC 0.96 and accuracy 86.0%.
Limitation
Retrospective, single-institution data with a small test set; only 127 real Garden I/II radiographs. A research model, not deployed clinically.

AI evaluation · MSK  |  J Imaging Inform Med 2024 · Co-author; ground-truth reader

Are saliency maps trustworthy explanations?

Clinical problem
Heatmaps are widely used to show “where the AI looked,” but their reliability had not been tested on musculoskeletal radiographs.
My contribution
Co-author, and one of the three fellowship-trained MSK radiologists who drew the ground-truth boxes, on a Johns Hopkins and University of Maryland study led by Venkatesh (first author) and Yi (senior author).
Approach
Networks trained on the public Stanford MURA upper-extremity dataset. Six gradient-based saliency methods were scored on localization, repeatability, reproducibility and sensitivity to model weights, against consensus boxes from three fellowship-trained MSK radiologists.
Published finding
Every method localized abnormalities worse than radiologists (AUPRC 0.438–0.590 vs. 0.816) and was less repeatable and reproducible. Five passed the weight-sensitivity test, but none met all four criteria. The authors recommend caution before clinical use.
Limitation
One public dataset of upper-extremity radiographs, and gradient-based methods only. Results may differ for other explanation methods, body parts or modalities.
